# Mondom

(GitHub repo: `stamasg84/mondom`, renamed from `beszelo`; the local folder is still `beszelo`. App name: **Mondom** ("I say it"), chosen because it has no accents and is pronounceable internationally. Application ID: `io.github.stamasg84.mondom`. Never change it after publishing.)

Free, ad-free, open-source Android speech aid (AAC) for literate adults who lost their speech (ALS, especially bulbar; also stroke, laryngectomy, MS). Hungarian-first.

## Why
Existing apps (Cboard, Speech Assistant AAC, Spoken, Proloquo4Text) rely on the system TTS (Spoken and Speech Assistant AAC can also use ElevenLabs cloud voices, which needs an account + internet; see `docs/TODO.md` "Optional ElevenLabs voice"), and Hungarian only has Google's female voice or robotic eSpeak. A natural male voice is possible (sherpa-onnx + Piper), but setting it up is too hard for families. Most users don't even know that the AAC app and the system TTS engine are separate things, so "it speaks in a female voice" looks like a fact about the app, not a setting anyone could change. Main differentiator: **manageable setup: one install, pick a voice in the app, works offline after that, Hungarian phrases preloaded, nothing paywalled.** All the TTS complexity is hidden; the user only installs the app, opens it, and picks a voice from a short list (e.g. Imre / Berta / Android alapértelmezett), with a sample to listen to for each.

## Core features (MVP)
- **Sentence lists** instead of tiles: everything the user can say with one tap lives in a list of whole sentences. The preloaded everyday phrases ("Kérek vizet!", "Éhes vagyok.", "Segíts felállni!", "Várj, gépelek.") are one list; the user adds more (e.g. "Orvos", "Éjszaka"). See `docs/architecture.md` "Sentence lists".
- Free-text area with a speak button on the main screen (not hidden in a menu); pressing it again with the same text repeats it instantly (no separate repeat button); plus history
- Offline Hungarian male (`hu_HU-imre`) and female (`berta`, `anna` if licensed) Piper voices, downloaded in-app; system TTS as an always-available option; other languages via the catalog
- Message banking: record your own voice for any sentence, played back in place of TTS. With ALS there's often a window between diagnosis and losing speech, and recordings made then are the most personal option. Plain on-device recording, no accounts, nothing paywalled (Cboard puts this behind a sign-in).
- **Prepared conversations** ("Beszélgetés"): write the sentences ahead of time (e.g. the evening before a relative visits: "Ki kéne cserélni a villanykörtét.", "Légyszi vegyetek a boltban trappistát!", "Mi újság a suliban?") and say them one by one during the visit, with no typing and no awkward waiting. A prepared conversation is just a sentence list with "said" tracking and a "Következő" button. A differentiator: no other AAC app we've seen has this.
- **Optional ElevenLabs voice** (Phase 7, moved ahead of the release 2026-11-13): the user's own ElevenLabs account/key (e.g. their voice clone), cached, with automatic fallback to an offline voice. A whole list can be **generated into message-banking recordings** with that voice (decided 2026-11-14), so the user's cloned voice keeps working offline for good, even after the key or subscription is gone. See `docs/architecture.md` "ElevenLabs voice".
- **Alarm** ("Riasztás", decided 2026-11-05): a seeded, pinned list whose rows are sentences with a built-in sound ("Vészhelyzet!" → looping siren, "Gyere ide!" → one bell). Any row can be flagged as an alarm (played loud on the alarm stream, kept out of history), so a typed sentence or, later, a message-banking recording can be an alarm too. No extra button on the speaking screen. See `docs/architecture.md` "Alarm".
- **No 112 call / emergency SMS** (dropped 2026-11-05): users might rely on it instead of really calling 112, while Android limits what an app can do (no audio into calls, Play restricts `SEND_SMS`). See `docs/TODO.md` "Emergency".

## Later
Own word prediction only if users ask for it (the Android keyboard, e.g. Gboard, already predicts Hungarian words in Mondom's text field, so this isn't a gap), Bluetooth speaker and volume boost, large "show text" mode, Switch Access / dwell support as hand function declines, voice banking (fine-tune a Piper voice on the patient's own recordings; message-banking recordings could feed this), a better Hungarian male voice (fine-tune Piper on 1–3 h of expressive recordings from a volunteer/actor).

## Constraints / gotchas
- Voice licenses: `imre` and `berta` are MIT (checked); `anna` still to check. Swedish (added 2026-11-03 for testing a second language, and a tester in Sweden): `sv_SE-nst` CC0, `sv_SE-alma` CC BY 4.0 (needs credit in "About"). MIT is compatible with GPL-3.0 bundling, but keep the attribution/license text in the app's "About" screen. espeak-ng (used by sherpa-onnx as the phonemizer) is GPL-3.0, so Mondom should be GPL-3.0; sherpa-onnx itself is Apache-2.0.
- No accounts, all data stored locally. Network only for optional downloads (voices, the voice catalog) and the opt-in ElevenLabs voice (the user's own account, with an offline fallback); everything else works offline.
- Piper voices have flat intonation. To soften it: tune `length_scale` / `noise_scale` / `noise_w` in sherpa-onnx, and punctuate the preloaded phrases ("Kérek vizet!", "Segítenél?"), since Piper uses punctuation for intonation. Larger expressive models (e.g. XTTS-v2) are too heavy for phones and non-commercially licensed.

## Voice delivery & performance
- **No voices bundled in the APK** (decided 2026-11-01, replacing the earlier "bundle imre + berta" plan). One small APK serves every market. On first launch a picker offers the curated voices for the UI language plus **"Android alapértelmezett" (system TTS)**, which works offline immediately, so the app is never mute, even on bad hospital wifi. Piper voices (~60 MB each) are downloaded once and work offline afterwards. Each voice has a short sample (streamed) so families can listen before downloading.
- **Voice catalog**: `voices/catalog.json` in this repo, fetched from raw.githubusercontent.com, with a bundled copy as fallback. The repo is public (since 2026-11-01), so the remote fetch works. It is the curated allowlist (only listed voices can be downloaded, so only license-checked voices are offered) and holds URL, size, sha256, license and sample URL per voice. URLs point at sherpa-onnx's `tts-models` GitHub release; we don't host model files. Mirror them in our own releases only if needed (URL breakage, or `.tar.bz2` extraction too slow on cheap phones). Don't rename the GitHub repo once released apps fetch the catalog from it. F-Droid-friendly (no Play asset packs).
- Piper medium runs faster than real time on ordinary phones (estimated RTF ~0.1–0.3 on mid-range; not yet measured), so no strong hardware is needed.
- **No audio caching for now**: sentences are synthesized on tap. Generation on the Pixel 6a is under ~0.5 s, which is fine even mid-conversation, and skipping the cache saves storage and complexity. Pre-generating/caching WAVs is an optional fallback in `docs/TODO.md` if testing shows noticeable waits on cheap phones.
- Load the TTS model in the background at app start (loading takes ~1–2 s).
- Synthesis runs in a background isolate. Typed text is synthesized in one call, without splitting into sentences ourselves (the app isn't for long texts; streaming is in `docs/TODO.md` if cheap phones need it).
- **First technical prototype**: minimal app that speaks typed text with `imre`, tested on the cheapest Android phone available, to measure real speed.

## Stack
Flutter (Dart), sherpa-onnx via its official `sherpa_onnx` Flutter package (Piper ONNX models bundled; sherpa-onnx is the on-device runtime, Piper provides the voices, espeak-ng handles text→phonemes), local storage.

Flutter was chosen over Kotlin/Compose to keep iOS open later. Android is still the only target for now: don't spend effort on iOS until it's actually feasible. Platform-specific bits (emergency call/SMS, bell notifications, audio routing) go behind small platform channels/plugins so an iOS version only has to fill those in. Known iOS differences: SMS can't be sent in the background (the user has to confirm each message), and the App Store distribution model doesn't match F-Droid.

## Status
Validation phase.

Findings so far:
- **Cboard** (tried on Android): closest existing app. Picture + caption tiles, custom tiles and folders, custom text. Gaps: no free-text typing for saying something custom; speaks with the system TTS (female Hungarian voice); recording custom audio for tiles is locked behind a sign-in/"join". Cboard is open source, but targets picture-based communication (largely children); Mondom is typing-first for literate adults, so it's a separate app built on text sentence lists rather than picture tiles (Cboard's editing UX is still a useful reference).
- **Other competitors (web research, 2026-11-05):** **Predictable** (Therapy Box) is the closest: typing-first, aimed at ALS/stroke adults, Android + iOS, Hungarian word prediction, free ElevenLabs voices for its users, but **61 999 Ft** one-off on the Hungarian Play Store. **Spoken** (Android + iOS): typing-first, free download but premium is a subscription (~$13/month, $100/year, $250 lifetime), English-first, reportedly no offline use; ElevenLabs integration. **Grid** (Smartbox): Windows/iPad only, ~£550, clinic/eye-gaze device market. **Proloquo4Text**: iOS/macOS only, ~$120, no Hungarian. So on Android in Hungary the realistic choices are Cboard (free, symbol tiles), Predictable (expensive), Spoken (subscription) and Mondom. With the optional ElevenLabs voice added, Mondom would match the paid apps' main voice feature while staying free and offline-first.
- **Own use (2026-11-03):** after trying the speaking screen, it already feels much better than Cboard for ALS patients even without the extra voices (typing-first, whole-sentence lists, tap-again-to-repeat). So the pitch isn't only "a male voice": the whole app is free, no ads, no accounts, and message banking is free too (Cboard puts it behind a sign-in). Still to confirm with someone other than the developer.
- **ElevenLabs Impact Program eligibility for Hungarians (web check, 2026-11-14):** probably yes, not confirmed. Patients with permanent speech loss or change get a free, renewable 5-year Pro license (clinicians: 1-year Pro). You apply in a free ElevenLabs account (profile menu → "Apply for Impact Program", no card needed) by choosing an Impact Partner for your region and diagnosis. There is no Hungarian partner, but the **global** partners should cover Hungary: Bridging Voice (ALS/MND), Scott-Morgan Foundation and "11Labs – UCL" (all voice-affecting conditions; UCL is also the stated catch-all). No source names Hungary or rules out any country. To confirm, ask the UCL contact on the official page or try the application with a Hungarian patient. Instant cloning needs ~1–2 min of clean single-speaker audio; Impact volunteers also clean old recordings for people who can no longer speak. Source: elevenlabs.io/blog/expanding-access-patients-and-clinicians-can-now-apply-directly-on-the-elevenlabs-website.
- **Other markets:** Google's system TTS has only a female voice for Slovak, Romanian, Serbian, Finnish and Ukrainian; Piper has a usable male voice for Romanian, Finnish and Ukrainian (see `docs/TODO.md`).
- **Voices** (listened to the Piper samples): `berta` is about as good as Google's female voice. `imre` is acceptable but flat, "like an untalented actor reading from paper." Open question for users: is a flat male voice preferable to a lively female one?

Next: get to a usable Play Store release first (decided 2026-11-03): no dialog with the ALS group before they can actually try the app, since feedback on a working app is far better than on a description, and first contact shouldn't be spent on something they can't install. The features still planned are cheap, so this isn't far off.

After the release: ask in a Hungarian ALS Facebook group / speech therapists (logopédusok) what's missing. Play `imre` next to Google's female voice and ask which they'd rather use. Ask "did you know you can change the voice?" (expect mostly no). Also ask whether they use the one-shot "load into the text field" button (T19a) and building sentences from pieces.
